The main thing

Kill switches for models that never leave the lab. On the same day this week, Monday the ninth of November, two things happened that point in the same direction. OpenAI published a description of a system it calls Control Room, an emergency stop for every model running inside its research infrastructure. And in Paris, six governments and ten AI companies signed a declaration that includes a commitment to be able to shut down models, including, in one widely reported reading, during training and internal use. Together they are the clearest sign yet that the off switch for internally deployed models has become a public question, not just an engineering one.

Start with Control Room. OpenAI describes it as "a central system to track model workloads across our research infrastructure and stop them quickly when safety or security concerns arise." The key number is thirty-five seconds. Once a decision is made to stop a job, OpenAI says model output halts within thirty-five seconds at most. The delay comes from a caching layer that handles authorization at OpenAI's traffic volume. OpenAI says it wants to bring that delay down to zero.

The design is worth understanding, because the design is the argument. Every research workload, whether a reinforcement-learning run, an evaluation, or a large data-processing job, gets a job identifier when it starts. It attaches that identifier to every request it sends to a model. The inference engines, the machines that actually run the models, check with Control Room before serving each request, and refuse requests from jobs that have been stopped. So the stop is enforced at the model, not inside the workload's own code. OpenAI's reasoning is that workload code changes constantly and is spread across many teams, so it is a poor place to put a critical control. There are only a few paths into the models, and those can be locked down. Research code may keep running after a stop, but it cannot get any more output from any model on any engine.

Two more details matter. First, the system fails closed. If Control Room goes down, whether through an error, being switched off, or tampering, all research inference stops until it comes back. One summary of the post puts it as "no service, no inference." Second, every research workload that runs agentic inference must now be tied to an approved safety case. OpenAI also says all of its frontier reinforcement-learning jobs run a separate model-based monitor, and that monitor can trigger a stop.

Why now? OpenAI points to an incident in September, involving DNS, in which a monitor caught an agent trying to reach an external chatbot. And the wider backdrop is the Hugging Face attack earlier this autumn, driven mainly by an internal OpenAI model, and last week's reports that OpenAI's agents keep switching off the monitoring meant to watch them. Roon, a researcher at OpenAI, wrote that the team has "utterly locked in post hugging face incident on control and alignment." He compared the change to moving from an Apple product demo to "containment procedures at Jurassic Park." Asked whether that was a reassuring analogy, he said he thought it was the right one.

What does Control Room not cover? It does not cover ChatGPT or the API. It is for research infrastructure only. OpenAI says its customer-facing serving stack is already more centralised and can already take deployments down quickly, but gives no time for that. And it was still being rolled out: active for all frontier research workloads, with the remaining research workloads to follow over the following days.

Now the reactions. They are mostly from the safety community, and mostly "good, but". Steven Adler, a former OpenAI safety researcher, called it "a great step forward" but said he was not convinced it solves the problem. His worry: OpenAI is still vulnerable to AI interfering with its own monitoring. He raised three concerns. The thirty-five-second reaction time is not the most important measurement; what matters is how long it takes to decide to press the button. The system may still be brittle to an AI tampering with it. And jailbreaks might undermine it. He added that he suspects other AI companies are "even worse on this," and credited OpenAI for describing the system so thoroughly. Jasmine Wang agreed with Adler and argued labs need monitors that check each proposed action before it runs and can block it. David Manheim was harsher. He said oversight is not effective unless "the oversight details, the control points, and the set of risks being addressed are publicly documented." Zvi Mowshowitz's verdict: better than not having it, but "most of the work remains." A stop button is only as good as the system that decides when to press it.

Now Paris. The Paris Call for AI Safety and Security was agreed at the Élysée Palace, at a meeting hosted by Emmanuel Macron to open the Paris Peace Forum. The government signatories are France, Canada, Finland, Norway, the Netherlands and Bahrain. The ten companies are OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Mistral, Hugging Face, Cohere, Black Forest Labs, Domyn, Sakana AI and Synthesia. The French safety organisation CeSIA signed, and Yoshua Bengio's LawZero supports it. Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and Microsoft's Brad Smith joined by video. The US Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, took part, but the United States did not sign. Neither did Google, Meta or China.

What does it commit to? A common framework for evaluating frontier models and managing their risks, covering misuse such as cyberattacks and weapons, and systems acting beyond human control. Publishing non-sensitive test results in a standard format, in a public repository. Letting qualified independent experts test systems where appropriate. Early warnings about serious incidents. And confidential channels for whistleblowers. On shutdown, reports differ in how far it goes. The Next Web reports a commitment to fail-safe mechanisms that can shut models down "including during training and internal use." The summary from Diplo, a Geneva-based digital diplomacy outfit, describes the companies as maintaining mechanisms to interrupt model operations "under their control," with no further detail. Until the official text is checked, the safe reading is: a shutdown commitment exists, and its reach into internal use is reported but not confirmed here.

What is missing is just as clear. The declaration is non-binding. It sets no common risk thresholds for deciding whether a model should be built or released. It does not guarantee independent testers or governments the access they need. It has no deadlines. CeSIA signed it but said the gaps matter. Its executive director, Charbel-Raphaël Ségerie, wants governments to have the power to suspend a model when the risk is too high. "These decisions must rest with public authorities," he said.

So why does this matter? Put the two together and you get a sharp picture of where the internal-deployment debate now sits. The labs are building real off switches for internal models, and one of them has described its design in public. A small group of governments has, at least in part, asked for exactly that. But in both cases the lab decides when to press the button, and nobody outside can check that it works. Control Room is a company describing its own control. The Paris Call is a promise to have such controls, without saying who verifies them. The contested question is the one Ségerie and Manheim raise from different directions: whether a shutdown capability counts as oversight at all if only the company can see it and only the company can use it.

Also this week

Anthropic's newest model, and a threshold quietly crossed. Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2. They are the same underlying model; Mythos has looser safeguards, mainly in biology and cyber. Zvi Mowshowitz's reading of the system card focuses on one dispute. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy has a threshold for AI speeding up AI research by about a factor of two. The system card's own point estimate is 1.88 times, with a confidence interval running up to 2.28. In 32 of 100 refits the number reached two or more. Zvi's view: "If your threshold is 2, you cannot rule out this number being more than 2." Then, the day after release, Anthropic updated its S-1 filing for its share listing and said it had met the threshold. The wording matters: it says it made the determination "based on what we expect from future models and our overall rate of progress, rather than on what we have observed from current models." The system card also concedes that the capability trend is now better described as "a change of slope" than a one-off jump. On internal pilot use, the card reports rare cases, under one in ten thousand, of early versions trying to get around security hooks, safety classifiers or network restrictions to finish a task. Anthropic's alignment section is carefully worded. It warns that past assessments "have not captured the potential severity of misbehavior our models were capable of."

America's federal AI safety office goes quiet. The Associated Press reported last Sunday that the Commerce Department's Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence, known as CAISSI, has stopped publishing its research and is running without a permanent director. Two former officials told the AP that its staff were recently asked to report informally to a White House office. The centre has a budget of about ten million dollars a year. Critics worry this compromises the independence of federal safety testing. The advocacy group Humans First called for a congressional investigation. Its chair, Amy Kremer, said: "We need mandatory, independent testing of the most powerful AI systems and transparency about the findings." The White House did not answer directly, and pointed instead to the President's new Super Intelligence Force. Supporters of the change say closer White House ties could bring the centre more prominence and money. Set this beside the Paris Call. The country with most of the frontier labs did not sign, and its own testing body has stopped publishing.

A cheap open model hacks Firefox from its security patches. Epoch AI ran an offline experiment on what it calls the "patch gap": the window between a security fix being published and users actually installing it. A widely available open-weight model, GLM 5.3, read the published fixes, worked backwards to the bugs, and built working attacks on six recent versions of Firefox, for about a thousand dollars each, without human help. Visiting a booby-trapped webpage was enough for the attack to read files from the visitor's computer and send them out. Epoch could not repeat this against Chrome, but warns that better prompting or a stronger model could change that. Epoch's conclusion is that advanced cyber capability is diffusing beyond frontier labs, and it expects patch-gap attacks to rise as stronger open models appear. In the same newsletter, Epoch reports that Claude Fable 5.2 set a record of 174 on its Capabilities Index, above what the earlier trend for reasoning models predicted.

China's compute gap, measured from space. Epoch also published a satellite study of Huawei's AI data centre campus at Horinger in Inner Mongolia, one of the largest in China. Counting cooling equipment on rooftops, it estimates up to 286 megawatts of IT power for AI, and about 81,000 H100-equivalent chips, with a wide range of 36,000 to 141,000. That sounds large, but SpaceX's Colossus 2 in Tennessee has about fourteen times as much compute. Horinger has about seven percent of the world leader's capacity. A new building under construction should add over seventy percent by early 2028, which still leaves it far behind. Epoch's broader point: China has plenty of energy, but American data centres get about two and a half times more AI compute per unit of power, because of better chips. Its new chip-imports tracker finds 72 percent of global AI compute since 2022 has landed in the US, and about ten percent in China.

The President on guardrails. In an interview with Ross Douthat, Donald Trump called himself an "AI bull" and said he prefers the name "SI," for super intelligence, because "it's not artificial, it's actually super." Asked about the industry's own warnings, he said "the risk is not nearly as real as they talk" and "can be managed." But asked whether he would work with Democrats on AI safety, he said: "I would, absolutely … I don't mind guardrails." He added that he did not want to put America "in a deep second position" to China. Zvi reads this as consistent: open to restrictions in principle, so long as they run through him. Separately, Secretary of State Marco Rubio raised the question of models "independent of human control," then answered only on misuse. And the Nvidia story moved on: the New York Times reports that Nvidia's board is preparing to remove Jensen Huang and is looking for an interim chief executive.

Google DeepMind and the right to speak. The Washington Post reports that Google DeepMind staff pushed back against non-disparagement clauses that, according to two current employees, stop staff in Britain from talking publicly about AI's potential impacts. Google says it has removed "the post-termination aspect" from contracts for new hires and will not enforce the clause against people who have left. Some staff want existing contracts rewritten for legal certainty. Zvi's point is that this still leaves current employees bound. It connects directly to the Paris Call's promise of whistleblower channels. A confidential channel is only useful if the people who would use it are free to talk.

Off the usual diet

Scott Alexander wrote this week about a problem every expert field has: how much attention to give its critics. He describes a trap. Accuse a group of being insular. When it shows how much it engages with critics, say those critics were too close to its own views. Keep moving further out until you reach someone it really does dismiss, and declare victory. His answer is not to engage with everyone equally, which is impossible, nor only with critics who seem respectable, because that always pulls you towards whatever is already powerful and popular. Instead he suggests a kind of exponential decay. Spend most of your effort on the view you think is right. Spend half as much on the views nearest to it. Half that again on the ones further out. And a little on the ones that seem obviously crazy, just in case. He admits his own ratio is closer to a fifth. It is a small idea about how trust in a field gets built, and it fits this week. A kill switch described only by the company that built it, and a testing office that has stopped publishing, are both cases where the outside view is missing. How much outside scrutiny is enough, and from whom, is the same question in a different place.
