The main thing

Should a regulator be able to block a model before a lab even uses it in-house? A pacing debate shifts to internal deployment.

For two months the main argument about slowing frontier AI has been about compute. The idea, sometimes called compute reallocation, is that labs would be required to move a share of their research compute away from making models more capable and towards safety. This week one of the people who first argued for that publicly changed his mind. Evan Hubinger, who leads alignment stress-testing at Anthropic, posted on LessWrong late on Monday that he wants to "(somewhat) retract" his endorsement of compute-based pacing. He now thinks the main thing to push for is giving regulators the power to block internal deployments of new frontier models. Zvi Mowshowitz picked it up in his Thursday roundup, and a business site ran it under the headline "Let Regulators Block Internal AI Use." Here is what the proposal says, where it came from, and the objections.

First, the proposal itself. Hubinger describes three steps. One: before a new frontier model is used inside the company, the regulator gets a review, perhaps a report from the lab, perhaps from evaluators embedded in it. He says the regulator would ideally be federal, but more realistically in the short term it would be California. Two: there is a short mandatory waiting period before the model can be used internally. He notes that the wait alone has a pacing effect, because it slightly weakens the loop in which each model helps build the next. Three: if the regulator objects during the waiting period, it can block the internal deployment for longer. Past some length, he suggests, it would need a court to agree. His example is that a block of more than three months would need court approval.

Why does he prefer this to compute rules? He gives nine reasons. The main ones are these. It is easy to explain. In his words, "it just makes a lot of sense that a regulator should have to approve before you can use a new model for a dangerous thing like RSI," meaning recursive self-improvement. Compute pacing, by contrast, is "sufficiently many steps from the actual thing we care about" that detractors can call it a random metric. It is well precedented, both in general regulation and in AI, where he notes the US federal government is already doing what he calls ad hoc blocking of external deployments. And it is a good moment for rulemaking. He writes that it has been hard to define good standards in advance, but it is often clear after a model is trained how dangerous it is. So the point just before internal use is, in his phrase, "precisely the point after you get most of the risk-relevant information but before you've actually imposed most of the risks."

He also claims it is easier to define. A compute rule needs fuzzy categories for different buckets of compute. An internal deployment rule only needs a definition of internal deployment. His suggested wording: "anything that meaningfully contributes to the company's operations or is not fully sandboxed training/evaluation." And it scales with political will. If will is high enough, blocking becomes an outright ban on using new frontier models for AI research.

He added a near-term version, credited to his colleague Sam Marks. At first the regulator could only set one uniform delay for the whole industry. Later, as it learns more, it could set delays model by model, depending on how dangerous, capable or hard to monitor each one looks.

The idea underneath comes from a post two weeks earlier by Anna Wang, titled "Delaying Internal Deployments Until After X Days of Safety Testing." Her target is concrete: "No checkpoint may be used internally for purposes beyond safety testing, until at least X calendar days after its hash is filed with an auditor." A hash is a short fingerprint of the exact weights. She suggests starting at ten days and possibly ratcheting up to ninety. She notes that the AI Futures Project had floated a much stronger version: AI research can only be done or assisted by AIs trained at least nine months ago.

What makes Wang's post useful is its list of ways to game the rule. A lab could register thousands of tasks from its real research backlog and call running them a capability evaluation. It could make every researcher an A/B tester, using the new model for normal work and calling that training. With a thirty-day wait, it could pull out a new checkpoint every day and "test" them all, so researchers always have a day-old model. And if each lab writes its own definition of "safety testing," competitive pressure pushes everyone towards the loosest one. Verification is hard too. The weights actually being served are often converted or compressed, so they will not match the filed hash byte for byte. Her near-term answer is spot checks by the auditor; her long-term answer is hardware attestation.

Now the objections, and there were real ones. Eli Lifland of the AI Futures Project said he had spent up to a week comparing compute caps with internal deployment lags. His view: "compute reallocation is similarly good or slightly better than internal deployment lags, but I'm not confident," and it would be best to do some of both. His main point is that a compute cap can be turned all the way to zero, giving something close to a pause, while even a full ban on using AI for AI research slows things less. A compute cap slows more in the near term; a deployment lag slows more over the long term. He also thinks compute caps are somewhat easier to verify. But he flags one consideration in favour of lags: they may make it harder for AI systems to interfere with humans aligning the next generation.

Alek Westover raised a deeper worry about tying blocking to risk assessment. He argues we do not currently know how to make a compelling case that a dangerous model is safe, even in theory. Current safety reports, he says, rest on ad hoc, subjective judgements, and often find evidence compatible with high risk and then conclude the model is fine anyway. If blocking depends on those judgements, he expects the pressure to keep going will usually win. He also points out that the question "will this model cause harm?" is the wrong one, if the research it does gets stolen and used elsewhere.

Oliver Habryka, who runs LessWrong, named what he sees as the most likely failure for any pacing proposal: teams inside labs insisting on large budgets for "safety research" that is barely distinguishable from capabilities work. He is only optimistic about proposals without big safety carve-outs, such as forcing 95 percent or more of compute to be served to outside customers, which he says is straightforward to verify. On the other side, Adam Karvonen agreed strongly with Hubinger. He argues that much algorithmic progress is really better data, generated by the best current model, so you cannot make next-generation data with an old model. Limiting which model can be used internally therefore also limits algorithmic progress.

Hubinger's own list includes the main objection to his idea: it can create an algorithmic overhang, where new techniques pile up unused and could be stolen. He thinks that is overstated, because blocking also slows the invention of new techniques.

Why this matters for governance. Three points. First, this is a call for prior approval of internal use, not just disclosure of it. Most existing frameworks, including the EU's general-purpose AI Code of Practice and California's SB 53, focus on reporting and safety frameworks; neither gives a regulator a veto over what a lab does with its own model before release. Second, the definition question is not as easy as Hubinger suggests. Wang's list shows that "safety testing" versus "use" is exactly where the line gets drawn and fought over. Third, everyone in the thread, including the proposal's supporters, agrees it depends on a regulator with the technical capacity to judge. Hubinger's argument is that forcing the regulator into the process is the best way to build that capacity. His critics' worry is that it will not be built fast enough.

Read Hubinger's comment and Wang's post together. Hubinger's is about a five-minute read; Wang's is about fifteen, and the open questions section is the most useful part.

Also this week

The Riemann Hypothesis, with Claude. Late last week, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, announced that he and Claude had proved the Riemann Hypothesis, the most famous open problem in mathematics, which concerns how prime numbers are distributed. He says the method also resolves the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. According to coverage in the Guardian and Zvi Mowshowitz's write-up on Wednesday, two groups, from the Lean and Mathlib formal-proof communities, have verified the formalised proof. Alpöge wrote that near the finish line "Claude far outpaced me." Zvi reports that Anthropic has since used Claude to shorten the formal proof sharply. Forecasts had placed this far later. Zvi quotes Terence Tao, thirty-five days before the announcement, saying the problem needs "something beyond current AI… maybe 2050." Eliezer Yudkowsky's reaction is the governance angle: the methods should generalise to making AI very good at anything that can be definitely checked, and "unfortunately that category includes 'make AI better at making AI'." He compared it to a company announcing a million tons of fifteen percent enriched uranium: not weapons grade, but a sign of where the process line leads. Prediction markets on P versus NP being resolved before 2028 reportedly jumped from about five percent to over fifty.

METR on what OpenAI's perplexity numbers do and do not show. On Tuesday, METR published its reading of the chart behind Jakub Pachocki's perplexity proposal, covered on Wednesday. The headline number: on OpenAI's internal codebase dataset, pretraining loss has been falling about seven times faster since early 2026 than from 2022 through 2025. At that pace, OpenAI would cover the drop from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4.1, which took three years, in about six and a half months. METR says plainly that its staff have no consensus on what this means. The puzzle is that benchmark scores, as tracked by Epoch's capabilities index, show no comparable jump. It may be overfitting; it may be that benchmarks are the better measure; or perplexity may be the deeper signal, with real-world capability gains to follow. METR asks labs for four things: loss on other held-out datasets, ideally kept by a third party; the dates pretraining actually ended; how compute and labour spending changed; and enough context to tell how much AI itself drove the gains. It notes Microsoft has already published its own numbers on OpenAI's dataset.

Washington moves on recursive self-improvement. Representatives Josh Gottheimer, a New Jersey Democrat, and Nathaniel Moran, a Texas Republican, have introduced the RSI Safety Act, H.R. 11157. According to Politico, as summarised by Zvi Mowshowitz, it would require the largest labs to report quarterly on how much of their research is done by humans and how much by AI. Separately, Representative Lori Trahan released an updated summary of her FRONTIER Act, which reportedly treats automated AI research as a catastrophic risk. Senator Cory Booker called for Congress to "rein in the race to recursive self-improvement, require reporting on compute allocations and algorithmic progress," and set up verifiable shutdown protocols. Senator Chris Van Hollen proposed independent evaluation of AI companies' models and practices. Meanwhile, according to TechCrunch as summarised by the AI Governance Institute, Dario Amodei on Wednesday called suggestions that Anthropic supports surrendering human control to AI "absurd." He also wrote that "we may want limits on this process," and that Anthropic does not expect it to be safe for Claude to build successive frontier models fully autonomously, "without meaningful human supervision or understanding." The obvious question is what meaningful supervision means when, by Anthropic's own account in June, AI writes more than eighty percent of the code merged into its codebase.

Who has disclosed what, and who offers to check it. Peter Wildeford put OpenAI's and Anthropic's recent disclosures about AI-driven research side by side, and suggested each company publish whatever statistics the other has. Some figures: Anthropic's estimate of AI-caused acceleration is 1.88 times, with a confidence interval of 1.62 to 2.28. METR's outside estimate is 1.9 times, with about eighty percent attributed to AI. Anthropic reports about thirty thousand agents running at once, with 0.002 percent of actions blocked. OpenAI reports median spending of 2,400 dollars a day per researcher on agents, and a dated internal target of an automated AI researcher by March 2028. Wildeford's caveat is that the definitions differ, so compare with care. OpenAI's numbers are all self-reported. On that point, Chris Painter of METR posted that METR "stands ready to investigate and verify any claims about pausing training/development/algorithmic improvements," if companies invite it. His terms include private access to staff and publication of METR-written reports. And the access must be robust enough that misleading the verifier would need the cooperation of a handful of staff from different parts of the company.

The Defense Production Act and Nvidia's chief executive. The Washington Post reported on Wednesday, citing three people, that Pentagon officials including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth floated using the Defense Production Act as leverage in the campaign to get Jensen Huang reinstated as Nvidia's chief executive. The law gives no clear path to installing a CEO. But one administration official told the Post it could be used to force Nvidia to shift production to military projects, which would hurt its plans and profits. The Pentagon denies the conversations happened. Separately, President Trump has said he is open to the government taking a stake in Nvidia. Pete Harrell of Georgetown told the Post the administration is "not really interested in setting out … regulations. It's interested in getting five companies in a room and kind of telling them what to do." For governance, the point is about who holds leverage over the compute supply. Informal pressure of this kind sits entirely outside the formal oversight frameworks being debated in Congress.

Off the usual diet

Hubinger's design has a close cousin in drug regulation. In the United States, before a company can test a new drug in people, it files an Investigational New Drug application with the Food and Drug Administration. It may start trials thirty days after the FDA receives the file, unless the FDA places what is called a clinical hold. A hold can be partial or complete, and it stays until the company fixes the problem. Silence means go. The point of that design is that it puts the burden of acting on the regulator, but gives the regulator a fixed window and the information to act in. Merger control works the same way. Under the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act, companies must notify the antitrust agencies and wait thirty days before closing. If the agencies want more, they issue a "second request," which extends the wait until the companies comply. Two lessons carry over. First, the short default wait mostly works as a trigger for information, not as a brake. Second, the real power lies in what the regulator can demand during the wait, and how hard that demand is to game. Both regimes have spent decades fighting over the same definitional edges Wang lists: what counts as a trial, what counts as closing.
