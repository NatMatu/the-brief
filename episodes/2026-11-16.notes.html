The main thing

Is AI really doubling the pace of AI research? The METR assessment, and its critics.

On the fifth of November, the evaluation nonprofit METR published two documents on Anthropic. Ten days on, they are still the most argued-over pieces of evidence in AI safety, so here is what they found, and what people have said against them.

First, the setup. From the twelfth of August to the twenty-ninth of October, a small METR team, about two and a half full-time staff, had what it calls "unprecedented access" inside Anthropic. That meant internal benchmark scores for unreleased models, training metrics, staff surveys, and around fifteen interviews with researchers. METR says it took no money from Anthropic, and worked on Anthropic devices for security reasons. The question was simple to state: is AI now speeding up the work of building better AI?

The headline answer is yes, roughly by a factor of two. METR's words: as of October 2026, Anthropic "is likely improving the capabilities of their models on well-specified tasks at around twice the rate they did between March 2024 and October 2025." The main evidence is Anthropic's internal version of the Epoch Capabilities Index, a composite of many benchmarks, which METR calls AECI. Before October 2025, Anthropic's models gained about fifteen points a year on it. Since then, about twenty-nine. That is a speed-up of 1.9 times, with a ninety-five percent confidence interval of 1.5 to 2.8. Put plainly, two years of the old progress in one year. METR adds that it "would not be surprised" if the true figure were closer to 1.3 or to 3.

Why does METR think AI is the cause? It says it is confident the speed-up did not come from faster growth in training compute. Beyond that, it is a judgment call. Its guess is that "perhaps" eighty percent of the current acceleration is due to AI, and it says that judgment carries substantial uncertainty. Staff interviews point the same way. One Anthropic employee said the company is "now only bottlenecked on ideas and compute, no longer bottlenecked on code." In informal internal polls, staff said the latest model made them about twelve times more productive. METR calls those self-reports "very loose evidence" and expects they overstate the real gain. It also found no clear bottleneck, in algorithms, compute or data, that would stop further acceleration over the next twelve months.

The second document is the sharper one for governance. METR reviewed the automated research section of Anthropic's August risk report. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy treats a doubling of the pace of progress, attributable to AI doing the research, as an early-warning threshold. The public report said acceleration was "less than a factor of 2." METR combined figures from the private, unredacted version of the same report and got 1.97. Its comment: a reader would take "less than a factor of 2" to mean meaningfully below two, so a better description "would seem to be 'around 2.'" METR's own analysis gave a range of 1.3 to 2.8. The private report itself called it "plausible" that the threshold had been exceeded. METR's verdict is that the public report "understates the level of acceleration," and that the threshold may already have been crossed by the fifteenth of July. It still agrees with Anthropic that the direct catastrophic risk at that point was low. But it warns it "cannot rule out a substantial further acceleration" over the next six months, which "could rapidly lead to 'high' risks."

Now the criticisms. They come from three directions.

The first is redaction. Anthropic had the right to strike any non-public information, and used it heavily. METR says the majority of the evidence it found most informative was removed, including forecasts for future models and the bottleneck analysis. Anthropic describes the redactions as aimed at information "that could accelerate other AI developers." One chart in the report is stamped "FAKE DATA" because the real one could not be shown. The result, METR says, is that the public must rely on its judgment "much more than would be ideal." Ryan Greenblatt, who works at METR, made the same point: conclusions beyond the raw benchmark trend "depend significantly on trust for the authors." Ajeya Cotra said she was "sad that so much of the underlying evidence in this report was redacted." METR was also meant to audit how Anthropic splits compute between capabilities and safety research. Anthropic cancelled that part midway, saying it wanted to focus on risk assessment first. Daniel Kokotajlo, of the AI 2027 scenario, responded that auditing compute allocation is the foundation for making any promise to slow down verifiable. One commenter on LessWrong listed the cancelled audit and tightened redactions as red flags, asking why information described as "very high-level" needed redacting at all.

The second is whether the number is too high. The 1.9 figure is Anthropic only. When METR adds models from OpenAI, Google and xAI, the industry-wide frontier figure drops to about 1.4. One explanation is that Anthropic lagged in 2025 and caught up, which would flatter its acceleration. METR itself lists ways it could be overestimating: non-AI factors such as new hires, the benchmark's focus on tasks with checkable answers, and an unusually slow Anthropic baseline in 2023 to 2025. Inside Anthropic, views differ. One researcher put AI's share of the gains at only fifteen to twenty percent; another said new hires were the biggest factor of recent months. On LessWrong, Julian Bradshaw argued the headline is "overstated" relative to true frontier acceleration, and that public Epoch data actually shows slight deceleration. J Bostock suggested Anthropic may have started optimising heavily for its internal metrics around Sonnet 4.5, so some of the jump could be teaching to the test. Another commenter pointed out that the chart starts in April 2024, near a known break in other trend data.

The third is the most interesting, because it cuts the other way on risk. Thane Ruthenis, also on LessWrong, argued the main chart is "kind of a bear signal." If AI were truly improving AI in a compounding loop, the slope should keep getting steeper. Instead the chart shows one change of slope, around the turn of the year, and then a straight line. That looks more like a one-off unlock than self-reinforcing progress. Bradshaw's reply: the leading forecasting model did not expect strong compounding yet, and METR mentions redacted evidence that acceleration may itself be rising. Even so, the disagreement is real, and it matters. A one-time doubling and the start of a feedback loop call for very different responses.

Finally, a disclosure from METR that drew wry comment. METR says Claude was "deeply involved" in most of the data it received, much of the documents, and most of its own analysis. In principle, the model being assessed could have shaped the assessment.

Why this matters for governance: this is the first time an outside evaluator has had deep access to a frontier lab's internal research process, and the first time one has said in public that a lab's own report understated a risk threshold. But it also shows the limits of the model. The lab set the scope, could cancel parts of it, and decided what the public saw. Read this. The METR summary takes about fifteen minutes; the full assessment is long, perhaps an hour.

Also this week

Jensen Huang resigns, and chip controls tighten. Nvidia's chief executive Jensen Huang has resigned and left the board. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim chief executive. This follows reporting that Huang had an affair with Alice Huang, a woman US intelligence determined to be a Chinese spy, whose company bought large volumes of Nvidia chips. The New York Times then reported a second relationship, with an executive who reports to him. The board had hired the law firm Wachtell Lipton to investigate. According to the Times, US officials told President Trump in October 2025, and he has said he has no regrets about not acting. On the twelfth, Trump said Apple's former chief executive Tim Cook should replace Huang. Zvi Mowshowitz notes Nvidia's market value has fallen about twenty percent. The policy consequence is the one to watch. Congress is negotiating chip export measures in the annual defence bill, including chip tracking to deter smuggling, and a role for lawmakers in reviewing sales licences. Speaker Mike Johnson and Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries both back including controls. House Foreign Affairs chair Brian Mast told Politico the scandal has "100 percent" weakened Huang's ability to lobby against them.

Anthropic's listing this week. Anthropic is due to price its share offering on Wednesday the eighteenth, and to start trading on Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker ANT. It is offering 825 million Class A shares at seventy to seventy-eight dollars each, which would raise up to about sixty-four billion dollars. At the midpoint, Yahoo Finance reports a fully diluted valuation of about 1.33 trillion dollars; other outlets cite 1.4 trillion, and CNN has corrected its own figure, so treat the exact valuation as moving. Bloomberg reports early orders at about six times the shares on offer. Two points critics raise. First, the share structure gives the seven founders a majority of voting power. Second, the filing discloses about 895 billion dollars in committed spending, mostly on compute and infrastructure. The listing lands in a falling market: the Nasdaq dropped five and a half percent the week before the roadshow. For governance, the questions are how public shareholders sit alongside Anthropic's safety commitments, and what a listed company must now disclose about risk. That last point is not abstract: the METR findings above, and Anthropic's own filing update on the research threshold, are now market-relevant information.

New York's frontier AI register is open. New York's financial regulator has opened the registration portal for the RAISE Act, the state's frontier AI safety law. Large frontier developers must register by the first of January 2027, when the law takes effect. Secondary sources say the portal opened on the tenth of November, and define the large tier as companies with over five hundred million dollars in annual revenue. Oversight sits with a new state office called DIGIT, inside the Department of Financial Services. From January, large developers must keep a current disclosure statement with that office and pay assessments to operate frontier models in New York. Frontier developers must also report critical safety incidents and publish transparency reports. Sources disagree on the reporting window; one says seventy-two hours, against fifteen days in California. Commentators note the office has not said how it will enforce against developers that fail to register. This is now the second US state, after California, with a working frontier model regime, and it is run by a financial regulator, which is unusual.

Off the usual diet

Scott Alexander argued this week that safety-minded employees at AI companies should organise, using the tools of labour law. His economics is worth hearing. Labour disputes are hardest where an employer can replace striking workers from a large pool. Top AI researchers are scarce and very expensive, so a dozen threatening to walk out already have leverage, with or without formal union recognition. Under US law, two or more workers acting together already count as protected "concerted activity." The game-theory point is the clever one. Negotiations between unions get less antitrust scrutiny than agreements between companies. So if one lab says it must build a risky design because a rival will otherwise, employee groups at both labs could agree to refuse together. That turns a race between firms into a coordination problem their workers can solve. He also suggests employee groups could hold companies to their own promises, such as hiring independent evaluators, or disclosing compute allocation. This connects directly to the main story. The compute audit Anthropic cancelled is exactly the kind of commitment he says nobody inside a company can currently enforce. And it connects to the antitrust suits filed in September, which claim that public calls for industry-wide coordination are themselves illegal. Coordination by firms invites antitrust attack; coordination by workers may not.
