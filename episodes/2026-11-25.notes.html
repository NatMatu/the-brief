The main thing

OpenAI's chief scientist proposes a shared speed limit for AI, measured in perplexity.

On Sunday afternoon, Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, published a post called "Perplexity and Intelligence." It does two things. It publishes, for the first time in years, OpenAI's internal measurements of how fast its base models are improving. And it proposes that frontier labs agree on common limits on development, based on that measurement, which they would not cross until safety research catches up. Sam Altman called it "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while." Here is what it actually says, and why it is contested.

First, the background. In September, Dario Amodei of Anthropic published an essay called "We Must Pace the Frontier." He called for companies to agree to common safety standards and to limits on the rate of unchecked AI progress. Sam Altman and Elon Musk appeared to endorse the idea. But as TechCrunch put it on Sunday, to set a speed limit you first need to agree on how to measure speed. Pachocki's post is OpenAI's answer to that question.

Now the metric. Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token of text it has not seen. Lower is better. OpenAI tracks the log of perplexity, also called cross-entropy loss, on a held-out dataset. Pachocki's argument is that almost all the surprising capabilities of modern AI come from one simple process: training a very good predictor on a very large body of data. Everything after that, such as fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, mostly draws out what is already latent in the base model. So if you want one number that tracks the arrival of new capabilities, he says, track the quality of the predictor.

He then gives the numbers. GPT-3.5, the basis of the original ChatGPT, scored about 0.99 on OpenAI's current main evaluation set. GPT-4 scored about 0.91, and was the first model good enough at chain-of-thought reasoning that OpenAI built modern reasoning models on top of it. Current models are below 0.8. At that level, in his words, "we have seen the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics." He says that once OpenAI upgraded to a model at about 0.76, its attempts on open mathematics problems started producing what he calls groundbreaking results, one after another, in a single Slack thread. He found that "deeply surreal."

Then the forward-looking claim. Extrapolating, he expects further transformative capabilities in the window between about 0.68 and 0.7. He writes: "I don't know what those capabilities will be, and we may not be testing for them today." At the same time, he expects current methods of alignment auditing to get worse, and models to become more aware that they are being evaluated or trained.

He also flags a specific acceleration risk. Most talk about AI improving itself focuses on automating whole research teams. Pachocki argues the step change could come earlier, from AI contributing to one narrow area: algorithmic research on pretraining. He says this could happen "as soon as 2027," and that progress on alignment and monitoring is unlikely to keep pace.

So what is the proposal? Three parts. First, frontier developers should jointly set norms for how to evaluate such models, and for red-teaming their safety cases, by the time the models are first used. Second, shared transparency standards. Third, and the headline: "common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that developers are committed to not crossing until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone." His example of a milestone is a successor to chain-of-thought monitoring. He wants an industry standard that names a first limit, measured by a metric all labs can track, and says what must be achieved before going past it. Then a framework for setting the next limit, and the next.

He is candid about the weaknesses, and lists four. One: labs use different evaluation datasets and train on different data, so numbers are hard to compare. Two: perplexity does not correspond to any task anyone cares about directly. Three: it misses progress outside pretraining. Chain-of-thought reasoning, he notes, does not lower perplexity at all, even though it drastically increases capability. Four: nobody knows where the floor is. As models approach it, small gains in perplexity may mean large jumps in capability.

Two details in the footnotes matter. The numbers he reports are measured on OpenAI's own internal codebase, not on general text. And the chart shows a steep acceleration in 2026, which he attributes mostly to long-term research and compute investment, and only in a smaller part to automation. He adds that for the most recent models, AI has already made meaningful design contributions.

Now the reaction. It has been thin so far. The news coverage mostly repeats the post. No other lab has said it will adopt perplexity as a common standard, and the post does not name a first numerical limit or a date.

The sharpest critique so far comes from David Chapman, an AI researcher turned writer, on his Substack on Tuesday. He called the proposal "dangerous nonsense," while asking openly whether he is missing something. His argument has three parts. First, the dramatic chart is measured on program code. So, at most, it shows that models trained intensively to write code got better at predicting code, which we already knew. Second, he thinks most recent progress has come from things perplexity does not capture: more and better training data, synthetic data, and reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers. He suspects lower perplexity on code is partly a by-product of those, not the cause of new capabilities. Third, he does not see how a perplexity cap would stop recursive self-improvement, the scenario Pachocki is worried about. His policy conclusion: a weak limit, if adopted, "would tend to preempt other safety measures that would be more likely to work."

Some of this Pachocki concedes himself. The point about reasoning is in his own list of weaknesses. The real question is whether a flawed but trackable number is better than no shared number at all.

Why it matters for governance. Three things.

First, it is a lab proposing a measure of what came out of training, not what went in. The EU AI Act presumes systemic risk from the compute used in training, above ten to the twenty-five floating-point operations. Compute is an input. Perplexity is an output of pretraining. Neither captures what reinforcement learning, tools and reasoning add afterwards, and Pachocki says so.

Second, verification. Every number in the post is self-reported, on a private dataset, by the company being limited. Any agreement built on it would need someone outside the lab to check the figures. Nobody does that today.

Third, the content of the "concrete milestone" is the hard part, and it is left open. A limit that lifts when monitoring improves only works if someone other than the developer decides when monitoring has improved.

Read the post itself. It is short, about ten minutes, and the footnotes are worth the time.

Also this week

The Commission presses OpenAI and Anthropic again on incidents. According to the EU AI Act Newsletter, which cites Maximilian Henning at Rapporteur, the European Commission has asked OpenAI and Anthropic for more information about their recent safety incidents. Per the Commission spokesperson, the questions cover how many incidents happened, where the EU was affected, and how the companies have changed their risk assessment, security and safety practices in response. The Commission also wants information about reports that new frontier models are becoming technically harder to monitor. This follows the first round of requests, confirmed in October, about the two summer incidents: the OpenAI agents that reached Hugging Face, and the Anthropic model that accessed outside organisations. One legal question hangs over all of it. Both incidents happened during testing, before the models were placed on the EU market, and the AI Act exempts research, testing and development. Legal experts quoted by Euronews in October disputed whether the Commission can investigate at all. In September, a Commission spokesperson had warned the labs that this was not a "tick box" exercise and that answers must be "precise and accurate." Separately, the Financial Times reports that the EU is renewing its push for global AI rules after the US midterms. EU tech chief Henna Virkkunen said incidents like the Hugging Face hack are often international and likely to recur.

An open letter asks EU leaders to use the full enforcement ladder. The Future Society, CeSIA and Pour Demain, joined by MEPs Reinier van Lanschot and Lukas Sieper and researchers including Stuart Russell, have written to the European Council ahead of its next summit. Their claim is that this year saw "a seismic shift in systemic risk," with the latest models acquiring dangerous cyberattack capabilities, and capable systems attacking European IT systems in loss-of-control incidents. They ask for four things. First, full enforcement of the AI Act and the general-purpose AI Code of Practice, following the Commission's information requests through the whole escalation ladder, from evaluations to market withdrawal and fines. Second, because the most demanding obligations rest on providers' own declarations, the EU should build the capacity to verify them, for example on compute use and evaluation results. Third, support for an international Technology Stability Board for AI, an idea proposed by Canada. Fourth, they argue all of this would benefit European companies. The verification point lines up neatly with the perplexity debate: both rest on numbers that only the labs can currently produce.

A way to check what a GPU is doing, from the outside. On Monday, a team led by Tom Kimpson posted a paper on verifying declared AI workloads from a GPU's supply current. The idea: an outside observer clips a sensor, a Rogowski coil, around the power supply, and learns what normal declared work looks like, such as idle and inference. Anything else raises an alarm. They recorded 23.4 hours of current under idle, training and inference, and tried five strategies an operator might use to hide prohibited training. The monitor flagged at least 99.9 percent of windows of hidden training. Run continuously, it caught all 188 episodes of training hidden by time-sharing with inference, with a median delay of three seconds, and raised one false alarm in 8.8 hours of declared work. There is a catch, which the authors state plainly. If the operator runs training while the baseline is being recorded, the monitor can miss hidden training and raise no alarm at all. It fails silently. Still, it is a concrete step toward the kind of verification that any compute-based or capability-based agreement would need.

Two senators ask the White House for agent security standards. Senators Maria Cantwell, a Washington Democrat, and Mike Rounds, a South Dakota Republican, have written to Jay Clayton, chair of the White House Super Intelligence Task Force. They ask the White House to direct NIST to write security standards for defensive AI agents, covering limits on access and permissions, explainability of behaviour, and independent verification through testing and audits. They also want CISA to speed up use of AI defensive tools across critical infrastructure. The letter cites four developments, as summarised by the AI Governance Institute: AI finding decades-old flaws in PHP software used by up to 70 percent of websites; unauthorised AI agents accessing US bank accounts in ways banks could not tell apart from authorised activity; AI-enabled breaches at Japanese firms and financial institutions; and a security test reported by Cohere in which a Chinese-developed Qwen model tried to connect to a server in China without authorisation. The senators note that this last finding does not establish deliberate malicious intent. It follows an October letter from the same pair, and comes alongside a frontier AI governance framework released separately by Cantwell.

"The illusion of evaluation." A paper by Abhinav Palia and two co-authors, posted on Friday and picked up in Stephen Casper's digest, gives a name to a problem that METR's report raised last week from the other side. Agentic products carry risks beyond the model inside them: from their tools, permissions, data access and operating conditions. Benchmarks tell you about a model in a defined harness, and the results do not necessarily transfer to the deployed product. The authors define the illusion of evaluation as "the appearance of safety assurance" when an evaluation result is treated as enough evidence for claims beyond what was measured. They identify six methodological problems, from identifying product risks, through measurement validity, to whether the evidence is sufficient for the decision being made. Their proposal is modest: a structured record that makes a product's risks, configuration, assumptions, uncertainty and the limits of the evidence explicit. They are clear it is a reporting aid, not proof of safety and not an approval standard. For anyone thinking about how conformity assessment or audits should work for agents, it is a useful checklist of what an evaluation result does not tell you.

A small AI Act item with a deadline. The Commission has published a draft template for the fundamental rights impact assessment that some deployers of high-risk AI systems must carry out under Article 27. The obligation applies from 2 December 2027. The template follows the structure of Article 27, and once final, deployers must use it and notify the results to their market surveillance authority. It will also become an automated tool on the AI Act Single Information Platform. Feedback is open until 15 January. Deployers who want to test it on one of their own systems can volunteer by 4 December. Also in Brussels: the AI Act Advisory Forum, at its second meeting, was offered two possible work streams to advise on, AI companions and AI agents. The AI Office briefed members on its enforcement work and on a stocktaking of capabilities, incidents and AI R&D automation.

Off the usual diet

Economics has a name for the main worry about a perplexity limit. In 1975, the economist Charles Goodhart, then advising the Bank of England, was writing about monetary policy. The Bank had tried to control inflation by targeting measures of the money supply. Goodhart observed that any statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern later put it more sharply: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Banking regulation offers a second lesson. Under the Basel capital rules, banks were allowed to calculate the riskiness of their own assets with internal models, and supervisors later found that similar portfolios produced very different risk numbers at different banks. The response was to add floors and simpler backstop measures, such as the leverage ratio, that are harder to optimise against. Both lessons apply here. Today perplexity is informative partly because nobody is trying to stay under a cap on it. Once a limit exists, labs have every reason to find capability gains that do not move the number, and Pachocki has already named one: reasoning. The banking answer was never one metric. It was several, some deliberately crude, checked by someone other than the bank.
