The main thing

California wants the power to cap recursive self-improvement. On Wednesday, Governor Gavin Newsom unveiled a report called "AI Safety in 2026 and Beyond." It comes from the California Government Operations Agency, is dated the sixteenth of November, and runs to about eighty-five pages. It is a set of recommendations to the legislature, not a law. But it is the most detailed plan any government has put out for supervising what frontier companies do inside their own walls.

Some background. In September, Newsom signed an executive order asking for recommendations in four areas: standards, independent verification organisations, incident reporting, and emergency shutdown. Four outside experts advised: Rob Reich of Stanford, Jason Goldman, the economist Gillian Hadfield, and Alondra Nelson. The report builds on SB 53, the frontier AI law Newsom signed a year ago, and on two laws passed this autumn, SB 813 and AB 1405, which set up a licensing system for AI auditors.

The report's starting point is blunt. It says the Hugging Face incident in the summer "was one of dozens of instances in which AI agents operated outside of developer control." And it names the thing it is most worried about. In its words, "the use of AI to automate AI research and development is growing, increasing risks exponentially." Companies already use their best models to build the next ones. If that loop becomes self-sustaining, the report says, it "could outpace human oversight and control."

So the first recommendation is about that loop. The state, it says, "must have an immediate and ongoing line of sight into the possibility of recursive self-improvement with the authority to cap its development and use if needed." Developers would report metrics on how much of their AI research is automated, including the share of compute they devote to it. They would send a dashboard summary to the state at least quarterly, and report any sudden change straight away. Verifying those numbers would be the very first job of the new independent verifiers.

Then comes the cap itself. The legislature should give the state "standby authority to cap automated AI R&D when the level of risk is unacceptable." The example given is a cap on the share of compute a developer can spend on automating its own research. The report is careful here. It does not recommend imposing a cap now. The power would kick in if a developer's automated research goes past its own committed thresholds, or advances faster than its safeguards and evaluations can keep up. Any cap would be limited in time, reviewed periodically, and lifted once a verifier confirms the problem is fixed.

The second pillar is oversight. Every large frontier developer would be supervised on an ongoing basis by a licensed independent verification organisation. Verifiers would need, quote, "full access to the people and information they need," and would share unredacted assessments with the state. If a verifier cannot attest that a developer meets the standards, the state could order corrective action. The developer could challenge that in court before it takes effect. A surcharge on verification fees would help pay for CalCompute, a public compute cluster with capacity reserved for independent safety research.

The report also pushes evaluation earlier. It says a powerful model can be dangerous before release, because its weights can be stolen, insiders can misuse it, and a misaligned model can cause harm during training or internal use. It reports "broad consensus" among the experts it consulted that a model should not be trained past a capability threshold, "deployed internally," or released, until the developer has shown its risks are acceptable and an independent party has confirmed it.

Third, incident reporting gets much wider. Under SB 53, most incidents must be reported within fifteen days. The report wants forty-eight hours, keeping twenty-four hours for imminent catastrophic risk. Attempts to steal model weights should be reported even if nobody is hurt. So should any loss of control, "whether from internal or external use of a model." There would be a near-miss channel, and whistleblower protection would extend to former employees and contractors. The report also wants to drop sole reliance on the ten-to-the-twenty-six compute threshold for defining a frontier model. It says that threshold kept the Hugging Face incident out of scope. It cites OpenAI's own internal-use risk summary, which says the models involved "fall outside the report's model scope."

Fourth, shutdown. The report stops short of a kill switch. Instead, every large developer would write a Risk Containment Protocol, tested by a verifier. It would show the company can intervene at several levels: compute, agents, internal access and deployment. It would also name who is responsible and plan how to work with cloud providers and power suppliers. It should test whether the model would try to block its own shutdown, for example by copying its weights. And if danger is imminent and the developer refuses to act, state officials could order an immediate emergency shutdown. The developer could seek a restraining order, but the order would take effect at once and stay in force pending review.

To explain why, the report uses the staff canteen. Before a tech company's café can serve a sandwich, it needs a health permit and inspections. California law even says deli meat must be stored at forty-one degrees Fahrenheit or lower. Yet the same company, the report says, can release a model that could hack critical infrastructure "without any license, without outside approval or oversight, and without passing any safety or security test established by law."

Newsom launched it in Washington, not Sacramento. "This is an action document," he told reporters. He called it "a template for how we should be doing it federally as well," and added, "This is not a Truth Social or a tweet."

Early reactions from the safety and governance world are warm. Charlie Bullock of the Institute for Law and AI called it "excellent, super useful and sharp." He said he did not expect to find a ban on recursive self-improvement "on page six, but I'm here for it." He noted that key design questions about the verifiers, such as who pays and who selects them, are left to the legislature. He had expected the shutdown section to be "kind of silly," and found it useful instead. Jason Wolfe of OpenAI, speaking personally, said he strongly supports many of the ideas and called them "very sensible responses to the current situation." Zvi Mowshowitz said it deserves extensive coverage and plans to return to it. One correction to the word "ban": the text proposes standby authority to cap, not an immediate prohibition.

What is contested is mostly politics. Newsom leaves office in January. His successor, Governor-elect Xavier Becerra, and the legislature, which returns next month, are free to ignore this. Both chambers held their own hearings last month. Newsom also vetoed SB 1047 in 2024, and coverage notes that this report takes up ideas he rejected then. The report itself sets aside liability reform and says little about open-weight models. Searches found no published industry pushback in the first day.

Why it matters. Most frontier rules so far govern what companies release. This plan reaches inside the lab: internal deployment, the share of compute spent on automating research, and evaluation before a model is even used in-house. It names internal deployment and automated research as things a state should measure and, if needed, limit. If California legislates even part of this, it becomes the reference point for every other state, and for discussions of the EU's Code of Practice too. Read the ten-page introduction; it summarises every recommendation.

Also this week

Anthropic goes public, and FLI goes after it. Anthropic listed on Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker A-N-T. According to Bloomberg reporting cited by Zvi Mowshowitz, the offering was about six times oversubscribed, and the final price was eighty-three dollars a share, about one and a half trillion dollars in valuation. First-day trading results were not yet available. The day before, the Future of Life Institute published a statement condemning Anthropic's pursuit of recursive self-improvement. The co-signatories include Steven Pinker, Rutger Bregman, Igor Babuschkin of xAI and Thomas Wolf of Hugging Face. The statement says: "We must curb Anthropic's political influence and reject its reckless bid for technological dominance." Max Tegmark wrote that Anthropic wears "two faces," warning of extinction in public while being "pedal-to-the-metal" on self-improvement in private. FLI cites the company's IPO filing as saying Claude writes over ninety percent of its own code, and that Anthropic "may imminently meet" its own policy's definition of dramatically accelerated progress. It also cites METR evidence of a nearly twofold speed-up. Epoch added a number on Wednesday. Using the filing, it estimates Anthropic spent nearly ten billion dollars on compute in the third quarter, up from four hundred and thirty-three million in all of 2023. About half of that went to SpaceX.

Alan Chan on when to pause. Alan Chan of GovAI published a short decision tree on Wednesday for when American frontier developers should pause. He assumes, for argument's sake, that a pause across all US developers is feasible. The first question is whether catastrophic harm is actual or imminent, meaning within a couple of months. If not, what would a pause buy? He lists closing gaps in a safety case, investigating incidents, improving oversight of automated research, security work, and time to pass regulation. Against that he weighs the American lead. He argues US developers currently take more precautions than their closest Chinese competitors, so a lead can let safety practices spread. His verdict, as of November: "a lengthy pause would be premature." The strongest case today is for short pauses to address specific problems, such as investigating a serious incident or putting in a particular safeguard. That fits neatly with California's report, which also prefers narrow, time-limited interventions. It is about nine hundred words.

OpenAI measures its own acceleration. OpenAI published a second report on how AI is speeding up its research. As quoted by Zvi Mowshowitz: at list prices, the median researcher now uses about two thousand four hundred dollars a day of agentic AI. The ninetieth percentile uses about twenty-three thousand dollars. Both are up sharply since September. "Agent workdays" now outnumber human workdays by about eight to one in pre-training and post-training, and five point seven to one across research overall. The median researcher said they would give up forty percent of their team's headcount rather than go back to GPT-5.2. Without coding agents, teams said, a month's work would take a median of three months. The report also says agents have "poor taste in research ideas, code quality, data quality, and writing," and need close human involvement. Zvi called it "a data dump of metrics rather than an attempt to do an investigation and tell a story." Put next to California's report, this is exactly the kind of number the state wants reported every quarter.

Outages, patches and the end of the defenders' window. This week brought a run of outages: an NSA internal network, Apple services, Google mail, and a Chase banking glitch on Monday. Each came with assurances that it was not a cyberattack. The leading explanation, reported by the Washington Sun, is that Wall Street executives and the NSA told Congress the outages came from rushing to patch vulnerabilities AI models had found. One briefed congressional official said: "They're trying to push through a lot of patches all at once — more than the system was designed for." Separately, Google's Threat Intelligence Group reported that attackers now use AI to read software updates, work out which flaw each patch fixes, and build an exploit before companies install the fix, all without a human. "Defending against machine-speed threats at human speed isn't feasible," Google said. Zvi Mowshowitz drew the policy point. He agreed with a researcher that the main medium-term threat is "rogue or misaligned internal deployments," which could corrupt a training pipeline. That is the same thing California's report is trying to see.

Off the usual diet

Scott Alexander wrote on Thursday about where AI forecasting goes next. His starting claim: AI forecasters now match or beat top humans on ordinary forecasting questions. He cites ForecastBench and the Metaculus Cup, and says general models are about six months behind the specialist systems. The interesting part is what is left. Prediction markets can only ask questions that resolve, because someone has to be paid out. So they are bad at conditional questions, such as "if this policy passes, what happens to that outcome?" They cannot answer causal ones at all. AI forecasters have no payout problem, so you can ask them anything. But according to researchers at the Forecasting Research Institute, AIs a year ago did noticeably worse on conditional questions. They expect improvement to be gradual, not a sudden switch. This connects to information markets. A regulator deciding whether to cap automated research needs exactly a conditional, causal forecast. If we cap this, does risk fall, and what does it cost? Markets cannot produce that information. Whether AI forecasters can, and whether anyone would trust them, is an open question about how policy-relevant information gets made.
