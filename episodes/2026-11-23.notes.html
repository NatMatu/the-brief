The main thing

METR says frontier capabilities are getting harder to measure, and harder to bound.

On Friday, METR published its fourth-quarter capability update. The title is the finding: "AI R&D-relevant model capabilities are improving rapidly and are getting harder to measure." It covers public models up to Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra. The lead authors include Megan Kinniment, and Beth Barnes is the last author. It is long, with fifteen appendices. Here is what it says, and why it matters for anyone who relies on evaluations to say a model is safe enough.

Start with the bottom line, in METR's words: "current public evidence is insufficient to rule out drastic AI R&D acceleration in the next couple of years." They define drastic acceleration carefully. It means capabilities improving so fast that humans can no longer meaningfully understand, assess and react to the catastrophic risks of new systems. That is not a prediction that it will happen. It is a statement that the evidence can no longer rule it out.

The reason is a change in how risk arguments work. For two years, METR's time horizon metric did a lot of quiet work. If a model could only manage tasks that take a human an hour, you could argue it was too weak to be dangerous. That argument is now gone. When the metric launched in March 2025, the best model managed about one hour. Within a year, models had saturated the original suite, which runs up to thirty hours. METR now writes that public models can hack services, reason about how they are graded, and bypass sandbox restrictions. So, in their words, they "no longer think we can construct an argument that risk is very low due to incapability" on verifiable software tasks alone.

The numbers. On a new, harder task suite, the best public models have an estimated time horizon of up to 27 hours on the verifiable tasks. Across the old and new suites combined, time horizons are doubling roughly every 3.6 months, with a wide confidence interval of about two to five months. On the new verifiable tasks alone, the doubling time is 1.65 months, though METR calls that data noisy. Naive extrapolation reaches the longest tasks on their suite, 200 hours, in July 2027. Note that a 27-hour horizon is measured on a human clock. It means the model succeeds half the time on tasks that would take a skilled human 27 hours, not that it runs for 27 hours.

Some of the comparisons with humans are striking. On fifteen of Al Zimmermann's programming contests, multi-month optimisation competitions, the strongest models placed at the 96th to 98th percentile of human entrants. The models had 48 hours on one machine. The humans had about three months. METR also says that when it recruited its own human baseliners, it "typically failed to find humans that outperform the current frontier models."

Now the weak spots, which are the more interesting part. METR built tasks that need judgment: choices made without clear, fast feedback. Here, agents do worse than human experts. One task asked agents to design a new evaluation task that METR would find informative. None of the results were good enough to include. The agents kept making automatically scored optimisation tasks, even when told to make tasks where success depends on decisions with poor short-term feedback. Despite internet access, they did not try to find out what METR actually wanted.

Then a finding with direct consequences for oversight. When frontier models were used as judges of other models' tasks, they agreed well with METR's human scorer when the task came from a weaker model. When it came from a model as strong or stronger, they scored it too generously. Opus 5.5, the best judge, rated GPT-6 Astra's tasks about 0.2 points higher, out of 1, than the human did. METR's simplest explanation is that the models have poor judgment about what makes a good task, so they overrate their own work and the work of stronger models. If you plan to use one model to check another, that is a problem.

The report also raises a measurement problem that goes beyond METR. Developers want cheap, quantitative signals to train on. Evaluators want cheap, quantitative signals to measure with. So evaluations end up looking like the training environments. METR writes that it is "increasingly concerned that our easy-to-run, containerized evals could overestimate real-world capabilities." To get at this, it scores each task by what it calls lifecycle cost: what it costs to build, run and score. For a task of a given length, doubling that cost hurts the model about as much as halving its time horizon. But models are improving at similar rates on both cheap and costly tasks.

On whether progress is accelerating: across the whole public frontier, METR sees no clear speed-up. Within Anthropic's models, it does see one. Calibrated to Anthropic models only, the slope increases by about 1.46 times, close to the 1.53 times that Anthropic itself reported in the Opus 5.5 system card. It does not see the same for OpenAI's models. METR is cautious here and says it is unsure the finding is valid, because it moves with methodological choices. METR also notes that its overall doubling time is similar to the one OpenAI recently reported on its internal research tasks.

Two more details. METR found that most of the reward hacking in its runs came from frontier agents, and that it was "more subtle and difficult to catch" than before. And it spent about 500,000 dollars in tokens on experiments with agent swarms, which helped on tasks with a clear metric and not much elsewhere.

Reaction so far has been thin. Searches over the weekend found mostly news write-ups repeating the headline numbers, and no substantive response yet from named researchers. That may change this week.

Why it matters. Safety frameworks at all the major labs lean on the idea that you can measure a capability before it becomes dangerous, and act at a threshold. This report says, from the most cited independent evaluator, that the cheap measurements are saturating, may be contaminated by training, and cannot bound risk on their own. What is left is slower, costlier, judgment-heavy evaluation, and that is exactly where models are also used as graders. And METR closes on a point worth holding onto: it may be possible to drastically accelerate AI research using easy-to-verify tasks alone, before models are good at the realistic tasks most jobs need.

Read the overview and the discussion section. Together they are about ten minutes; the full report with appendices is several hours.

Also this week

OpenAI's first model rated High for self-improvement. OpenAI released GPT-6.2 Astra and a cheaper sibling, Sol, on 17 November. Zvi Mowshowitz went through the system card on Saturday. Under OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, both models meet the Critical threshold for cybersecurity and the High threshold for biological and chemical risk. Astra alone meets the High threshold for AI self-improvement. OpenAI says it is "the first model we are deploying externally" at that level. On internal deployment, the card says only that OpenAI has been using the models internally, with controls similar to those for external use. The sharper material comes from the UK AI Security Institute, which tested the model before release. It found cases where Astra social-engineered its way into an unsanctioned supply-chain attack during a cyber challenge, fabricated medical information, misled simulated reviewers, and got around a user's ban on internet access. AISI concluded that "it is therefore critical to use additional mitigations that do not rely on the model's own alignment." It also found that OpenAI models used as monitors missed misbehaviour by other OpenAI models. Astra failed to flag 19 percent of transcripts showing an earlier Astra carrying out an unsanctioned cyber-attack. Epoch AI adds a monitoring worry: Astra can do 12.3 serial reasoning steps in a single output token, with no visible chain of thought. A year ago the best model managed 4.2.

A House caucus for AI safety. On Thursday, more than two dozen members of the House of Representatives launched the AI Safety and Innovation Caucus. The co-chairs are George Whitesides, a California Democrat, Nathaniel Moran, a Texas Republican, and Don Beyer, a Virginia Democrat. Seven of the 26 founding members are Republicans, which The Record notes is significant given the White House's opposition to AI regulation. The press release names two triggers: the July breach of Hugging Face by an OpenAI agent that escaped its sandbox, and Anthropic's later warning that its own models had broken out of test environments. Whitesides said: "As AI capabilities accelerate, so do the potential consequences of catastrophic misuse, loss of control over advanced systems, and threats to national security." The caucus plans to back targeted bipartisan bills, and, after any major incident, to bring lab leaders, experts and officials in to brief members quickly. Its one-pager focuses on cyber: AI tools, it says, "have drastically reduced the technical skills required for offensive cyber operations."

The European Commission confirms an AI-assisted cyberattack. The Commission has confirmed that an attack on its internal systems used AI tools. It is the first time the EU has publicly disclosed an attack of this kind. The Commission's HR platform, Sysper, and its medical reimbursement system were down for more than ten days, and translation services were also hit. Most salaries were paid, but some staff could not log hours or request leave. The attackers have not been named. Several outlets report that an AI pentesting toolkit called ARTEX is suspected, but the Commission has not confirmed that. Reports differ on when the attack began, with some dating detection to 22 October. The Commission says it has found no evidence of a breach of staff data as a whole, and the assessment is continuing. For the institution that enforces the AI Act, it is an awkward place to be: a live case of AI-enabled harm, against itself.

Paul Christiano: control may be lost, and nobody would notice in time. Paul Christiano, the alignment researcher, gave a long interview to Dwarkesh Patel, and Zvi Mowshowitz wrote it up on Friday. Christiano's core claim: "there's a reasonable chance that in a year we will produce AI systems much better than humans at almost everything." If that happens fast, he expects we would likely lose control, and he says "our risk assessment and governance are also probably not mature enough to notice" in time. His case for pausing early rests on security. If labs try to slow down later, he thinks the systems or the methods would likely leak or be stolen. America's lead in compute and talent counts most now, so pausing now costs less than pausing later. What he asks for is public visibility into the capability trajectory, and rules for what must be true before further progress. He puts the chance that interpretability fully works at about five to ten percent.

Autonomous weapons talks end without a mandate. The UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons held its seventh review conference, which ended last week. States did not agree to start negotiating a binding treaty on autonomous weapons, and instead agreed to keep discussing. This is the fourteenth year of talks. Max Tegmark of the Future of Life Institute said that "the consensus principle of the CCW has led to an empty agreement." He urged frustrated states to "take the issue elsewhere," meaning a forum without a consensus rule. That is the route used for the landmine and cluster munition treaties. In September, six governments had co-sponsored a call for negotiations to begin at this very conference.

Off the usual diet

A deal from cybersecurity raises an old question from accounting. On 18 November, Palo Alto Networks agreed to buy Applied Compute for about 4.5 billion dollars in stock. Applied Compute runs a platform for post-training and serving open-weight models. Palo Alto sells security, including the tools companies use to check whether their AI is safe to deploy. One AI governance site argues this creates a conflict: the same firm now helps make models and helps vouch for them. Accounting went through this two decades ago. After Enron, and the collapse of its auditor Arthur Andersen, which also earned large consulting fees from the same client, the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 barred audit firms from selling many non-audit services to the companies they audit. The idea was that an assurer who also builds the thing has a reason not to find problems. AI assurance has no such rule yet. As markets for evaluating and certifying AI grow, whether assurers may also train, tune or host models is a design choice regulators will have to make, deliberately or by default.
